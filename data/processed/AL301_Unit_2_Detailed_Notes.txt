2. Forms of Communication: The Big Picture
Communication can be classified according to the channel used, the number of participants, the direction of information
flow and the degree of formality. In real life, several forms operate together: a teacher may speak, use slides, gesture
and invite questions in the same lesson.
Sender Message Channel Receiver Feedback
Main classifications
Basis Types Illustration
Mode / channel Verbal, non-verbal, visual, written Conversation, gesture, chart, email
Participants Intrapersonal, interpersonal, group, public, Self-talk, pair discussion, lecture, TV broadcast
mass
Formality Formal and informal Official circular vs friendly chat
Direction in an Downward, upward, horizontal, diagonal Manager instructions; employee feedback; peer
organisation coordination
Feedback pattern One-way and two-way Recorded announcement vs interactive discussion
Verbal communication
Verbal communication uses words, either spoken or written. Oral communication includes conversations, lectures,
interviews, meetings, telephone calls and presentations. Written communication includes emails, reports, notices,
letters, minutes and messages. Words provide explicit content, while tone, pace and context influence how the content
is understood.
Non-verbal communication
Non-verbal communication conveys meaning without relying only on words. Facial expressions, eye behaviour,
gestures, posture, physical distance, touch, voice qualities, appearance, silence and the use of time can strengthen,
qualify or contradict a verbal message.
Remember
Effective communication is not simply sending information. It is checking whether the receiver has
understood the intended meaning.

3. Verbal Communication: Spoken and Written
Verbal communication is useful when a message needs words to explain facts, instructions, opinions or emotions. Its
effectiveness depends on the choice of words, structure, tone, audience and opportunity for feedback.
Oral communication Written communication
Usually immediate and interactive. Can be planned, edited and stored.
Tone and instant feedback can clarify meaning. A permanent record can support accountability.
Useful for discussion, interviews and urgent coordination. Useful for policies, instructions, reports and formal decisions.
May be forgotten or misunderstood if not documented. Can sound impersonal; feedback may be delayed.
Principles of effective verbal communication
(cid:127) Clarity: use familiar words and a logical sequence.
(cid:127) Conciseness: remove repetition while retaining necessary details.
(cid:127) Correctness: verify names, dates, figures, grammar and facts.
(cid:127) Completeness: include what the receiver needs to act.
(cid:127) Courtesy: use respectful language, even when disagreeing.
(cid:127) Concreteness: replace vague claims with specific information.
(cid:127) Audience awareness: adapt technical vocabulary and detail to the listener.

Practical check
Before sending a message, ask: Who is receiving it? What action is needed? By when? What could be
misunderstood?

4. Non-verbal Communication (NVC)
Non-verbal communication includes signals that communicate meaning without words or alongside words. These
signals can show interest, confidence, discomfort, respect, urgency and emotional state. Their meaning is not universal:
the same gesture can be interpreted differently across cultures and situations.
Face Eyes Gestures Posture Space & voice
Functions of non-verbal cues
Function What it does Example
Repeating Says the same thing as words. Saying “this way” while pointing.
Complementing Adds detail to spoken words. A warm smile supports a welcome.
Substituting Replaces words. A nod may mean “yes” or “I understand.”
Regulating Manages turns in conversation. Looking toward a speaker can invite them to continue.
Contradicting Conflicts with spoken words. Saying “I’m fine” with a tense voice and withdrawn
posture.
Emphasising Highlights a point. A deliberate hand movement underlines a key idea.
Why NVC matters
(cid:127) It helps speakers appear attentive and approachable.
(cid:127) It provides clues about whether a listener is following the message.
(cid:127) It can make presentations more engaging and easier to understand.
(cid:127) It supports rapport, but should not be treated as a perfect way to read someone’s thoughts.
Important caution
Never judge a person’s honesty, interest or personality from one isolated gesture. Consider the context,
culture, individual habits and other signals together.

8. Language, Psychological and Organisational Barriers
Semantic barriers arise when people attach different meanings to the same words or symbols. Technical terms may be
precise for experts but confusing for beginners. Words such as “soon,” “adequate” or “urgent” can also mean different
things to different people.
Examples of semantic barriers
(cid:127) Jargon: specialist language that an audience may not know.
(cid:127) Ambiguity: a sentence with more than one possible meaning.
(cid:127) Different language proficiency: the receiver may know the topic but not the vocabulary.
(cid:127) Overly complex sentences: multiple ideas make the action difficult to identify.
(cid:127) Faulty translation: a translated phrase may not preserve tone or intended meaning.
Psychological barriers
Emotions influence both sending and receiving. A worried student may not ask a question; an angry manager may hear
feedback as an attack; a person who expects criticism may interpret neutral comments negatively. Stereotypes and
confirmation bias can also lead people to notice only information that supports their existing beliefs.
Organisational barriers
Rigid hierarchy can discourage employees from sharing concerns. Departmental silos may prevent teams from seeing
the same facts. Unclear roles can lead to duplicated work, while excessive formalities can delay urgent decisions.
Informal rumours may fill gaps when official communication is slow.
Problematic message Improved version
“Do the needful ASAP.” “Please check the attached attendance sheet and send the
corrected version by 2 p.m. today.”
“The report is wrong.” “The total in Table 2 does not match the figures in the appendix.
Please verify it.”
“No one told me.” “I may have missed the update. Could you confirm which channel
was used?”
A useful habit
Use the teach-back method for important instructions: ask the receiver to explain the next steps in their own
words, without making them feel tested.

10. Communicating in a Global World
Global communication happens when people work, study or collaborate across countries, languages, time zones and
cultural expectations. Online meetings, international supply chains, remote teams and global education make
intercultural competence an everyday professional skill.
Best practices for global communication
(cid:127) Use plain international English: avoid idioms, slang, obscure humour and unnecessary abbreviations.
(cid:127) Speak at a measured pace: pause between major points and pronounce key terms clearly.
(cid:127) Make writing scannable: use informative subject lines, headings, short paragraphs and action bullets.
(cid:127) Check understanding: summarise decisions and invite questions without embarrassment.
(cid:127) Respect time zones: rotate meeting times where possible and record key outcomes.
(cid:127) Be careful with humour: jokes, irony and sarcasm may not translate well.
(cid:127) Confirm dates and units: write dates unambiguously, such as “12 October 2026,” and specify time zones.
(cid:127) Use inclusive participation: give quieter members time and more than one way to contribute.
Example: global project email
Subject: Design review — action items due 16 October
Hello team,
Thank you for today’s review. Please upload the revised diagrams to the shared folder by 16 October,
17:00 IST. Add a short note describing each change. If the deadline is difficult, reply by tomorrow so that we
can agree on a plan. I have listed the decisions and owners below.
Best regards,
Project Coordinator
Digital etiquette in cross-border teams
Choose synchronous communication (live call) when nuance, discussion or rapid clarification matters. Choose
asynchronous communication (email, shared document or recorded update) when people need time to review
information or work across time zones. Document decisions so that team members who could not attend can still
contribute.

12. Listening Skills and Active Listening
Hearing is the physical process of receiving sound; listening is the active process of attending to, interpreting, evaluating
and responding to a message. Effective listening requires concentration and an intention to understand before replying.
Receive Attend Interpret Evaluate Respond
Types of listening
Type Purpose Example
Discriminative Notice differences in sounds, tone or Recognising a change in a speaker’s voice.
emphasis.
Informational Understand and retain information. Listening to lab instructions.
Critical Assess evidence and reasoning. Evaluating a proposal’s strengths and weaknesses.

Appreciative Enjoy or value the experience. Listening to a speech, story or performance.
Active listening behaviours
(cid:127) Give attention: reduce distractions, put away unnecessary notifications and face the speaker.
(cid:127) Use brief encouragers such as “I see,” “go on” or a natural nod, where appropriate.
(cid:127) Paraphrase: “So the main issue is that the instructions changed after the deadline.”
(cid:127) Ask open questions: “What part of the process caused the delay?”
(cid:127) Reflect feelings carefully: “It sounds frustrating to repeat the work.”
(cid:127) Summarise agreements and next steps before ending the discussion.
The 70/30 reminder
In a difficult conversation, aim to give the other person enough space to explain their view. The exact ratio is
not a rule; the goal is balanced participation and accurate understanding.

14. Persuasion: Influencing with Reasons and Trust
Persuasion is the process of influencing a person’s beliefs, attitudes or actions through communication. Ethical
persuasion provides relevant evidence, explains benefits, acknowledges concerns and respects the audience’s freedom
to decide. It is different from manipulation, which hides important facts or uses pressure unfairly.
Three classical appeals
Appeal Focus Example
Ethos Credibility and trustworthiness of the A qualified trainer explains the basis for a safety
speaker. recommendation.
Logos Logic, evidence, facts and reasoning. A proposal compares costs, timelines and measurable
outcomes.
Pathos Emotion, values and human relevance. A story shows how a service helps students feel included.
Know audience State claim Give evidence Address concern Invite action
A practical persuasion structure
(cid:127) Attention: open with a relevant problem, question or brief fact.
(cid:127) Need: explain why the issue matters to this audience.
(cid:127) Solution: offer a specific and realistic proposal.
(cid:127) Evidence: support the proposal with examples, data or expert reasoning.
(cid:127) Objections: acknowledge the strongest reasonable concern and respond honestly.
(cid:127) Action: state the next step clearly.
Example: persuade a class to use a shared study calendar
“We sometimes miss submission dates because information is spread across messages. A shared calendar would list
each task, its owner and the deadline in one place. We can test it for two weeks and review whether it reduces
confusion. If it adds unnecessary work, we can simplify it. Shall we try it for the next assignment?”
Ethical checklist
Is the information accurate? Are limitations disclosed? Is the request fair? Can the audience say no without
being punished? If not, revise the approach.

15. Negotiation Skills
Negotiation is a communication process in which two or more parties discuss differences and seek an agreement. It is
common in project planning, workload allocation, deadlines, salary discussions, resource sharing and conflict resolution.
A good negotiation protects essential interests while exploring solutions acceptable to both sides.
Positions versus interests
Term Meaning Example
Position What a party says it wants. “I need the report by Friday.”
Interest The reason behind the request. “I need time to verify the figures before Monday’s
review.”
Option A possible solution. Submit a draft Friday and verified final version
Monday.
Objective criterion A fair standard used to evaluate options. Policy, workload data, agreed quality standards.
A five-stage negotiation process
Prepare Explore needs Generate options Evaluate Agree & review
Principles of constructive negotiation
(cid:127) Prepare goals, facts, priorities, alternatives and the limits of what you can accept.
(cid:127) Separate the people from the problem; criticise the issue, not the person.
(cid:127) Ask questions to understand needs before proposing a solution.
(cid:127) Generate more than one option rather than arguing over a single demand.
(cid:127) Use fair criteria and explain the reasoning behind your proposal.
(cid:127) Confirm who will do what, by when, and how the agreement will be reviewed.
Useful phrase
“I understand why that date matters. Could we explore a staged submission that protects your review time
and gives us enough time for quality checks?”

Conflict is not always harmful. When handled respectfully, it can reveal unclear expectations and produce better
decisions. It becomes destructive when participants attack character, withhold information, threaten, interrupt or treat
compromise as defeat.

De-escalation steps
(cid:127) Lower the pace and volume of your voice.
(cid:127) Describe the observable issue rather than assigning motives.
(cid:127) Acknowledge the other person’s concern without pretending to agree.
(cid:127) Identify the shared goal, such as accuracy, fairness or student learning.
(cid:127) Offer two or three realistic options and invite the other person’s view.
(cid:127) Write down the agreed actions and follow-up date.
Example: workload disagreement
Instead of: “You always give me extra work at the last minute.”
Try: “Three additional tasks came in after Tuesday’s schedule was finalised. I can complete the report today or prepare
the presentation first. Which is the priority, and can we agree on a process for late requests?”
Boundary reminder
Being polite does not mean agreeing to everything. State limits respectfully and offer alternatives when
possible.

A bad-news message communicates information the receiver may find disappointing, inconvenient or upsetting: a
rejected request, a delay, a policy change, a complaint outcome or an unsuccessful application. The communicator’s
task is to be truthful, considerate and clear while preserving professional relationships.

(cid:127) Be accurate and timely; do not hide the decision behind vague wording.
(cid:127) Explain the reason at an appropriate level of detail without blaming or exposing private information.

(cid:127) Avoid false hope, excessive apologies or promises you cannot keep.

18. Bad-News Message: Models and Examples
Example A: Direct message about a missed deadline
Subject: Revised submission date
Hello team,
The final report will not be ready for today’s 3 p.m. review because the verification stage is incomplete. The
review has been moved to 10 a.m. tomorrow. Please use today to check the figures and upload any
corrections by 6 p.m. I will share an updated agenda this afternoon.
Thank you for your cooperation.
Why it works: the decision and new timing are clear; the reason is brief; the next action is specific.
Example B: Indirect message declining a request
Subject: Request for additional lab slot
Dear Student,
Thank you for explaining your request for an additional lab slot. The current timetable has all available
laboratory periods assigned, and the room must remain available for scheduled practicals. For this reason, I
am unable to approve an extra slot this week. You may attend the next available supervised practice
session on Monday, subject to confirmation. Please contact the lab coordinator if you need help identifying a
suitable group.
Regards,
Course Coordinator
Why it works: it acknowledges the request, gives a neutral reason, states the refusal plainly and offers a realistic next step.
Example C: Giving constructive negative feedback
“Your presentation topic is relevant and your opening is engaging. At present, the slides contain too much text, so the
main argument is difficult to follow. Please reduce each slide to key phrases, add one example to support your main
claim, and rehearse within the allotted time. We can review the revised version on Thursday.”
Common mistakes to avoid
(cid:127) Being so indirect that the reader cannot identify the decision.
(cid:127) Using accusatory phrases such as “You failed to…” when a neutral description will do.
(cid:127) Giving a reason that is untrue or revealing confidential details.
(cid:127) Ending without a next step when a useful alternative is available.

Verbal communication Uses words; oral and written forms each have strengths and limitations.
Non-verbal communication Includes kinesics, oculesics, proxemics, haptics, paralanguage, chronemics, appearance and
silence.
Functions of NVC Repeats, complements, substitutes, regulates, contradicts or emphasises verbal messages.
Barriers Physical, semantic, psychological, physiological, cultural, organisational, technological and
overload.
Culture & global Use plain language, clarify expectations, respect difference and avoid stereotypes.
communication
Interpersonal communication Empathy, respect, assertiveness, feedback and adaptability support relationships.
Listening Receive, attend, interpret, evaluate and respond; paraphrase and ask clarifying questions.
Persuasion Use credibility, logic and appropriate emotional relevance ethically.
Negotiation Explore interests, generate options, use fair criteria and document agreements.
Bad-news messages Be truthful, clear and considerate; choose a direct or indirect structure based on context.
